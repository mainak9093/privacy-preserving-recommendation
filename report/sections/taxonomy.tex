\section{A Taxonomy of Approaches}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

Work in this area is routinely compared as though the systems were alternatives to one another.
They frequently are not: they protect different stages of the pipeline, under different trust
assumptions, offering guarantees that are not of the same kind. A system requiring two
non-colluding servers is not simply ``weaker'' than one requiring none, and a differentially
private recommender is not a slower or faster version of a cryptographic one.

This section proposes four axes along which any system in the field can be placed, and then places
the surveyed systems on them. The axes are what make the comparisons in
Sections~\ref{sec:training} and~\ref{sec:retrieval} honest.

\subsection{Axis 1: the trust model}
\label{sec:taxonomy:trust}

\emph{Who must be trusted, and not to do what?}

\begin{description}
  \item[Single server.] No non-collusion assumption. Achieved with homomorphic encryption or
        single-server PIR, and paid for in server computation. SimplePIR~\cite{simplepir2023},
        Spiral~\cite{spiral2022} and \panther{} sit here, as does
        \tiptoe{}, whose authors state that its ``privacy guarantee is based on cryptography
        alone; it does not require hardware enclaves or non-colluding servers''.
  \item[Two or more non-colluding servers.] The database or the secret state is replicated or
        shared across parties assumed not to collude. This buys dramatically cheaper protocols:
        multi-server PIR~\cite{chor1995,hafiz2019}, two-server machine
        learning~\cite{secureml2017}, three-party computation~\cite{securenn2019,araki2016}, and
        the systems of Section~\ref{sec:training:mpc}. The assumption is about the world, not
        about mathematics, and it is the single most consequential choice in this taxonomy.
  \item[Federated / user-side.] No server holds raw data, and users compute locally and upload
        updates~\cite{ammadudin2019}. The guarantee is weaker than it first appears, as
        Section~\ref{sec:training:fed} explains.
  \item[Trusted hardware.] Computation runs inside an enclave. Out of scope here
        (Section~\ref{sec:intro:scope}), and listed only for completeness.
\end{description}

These are not orderable by strength. They are different assumptions, and the right question is
which is plausible for a given deployment.

\subsection{Axis 2: the privacy notion}
\label{sec:taxonomy:notion}

\emph{What does ``private'' formally mean?}

\begin{description}
  \item[Cryptographic.] Security is defined by the real/ideal simulation
        paradigm~\cite{lindell2017,evans2018}: a protocol is secure if whatever an adversary
        learns from its execution could be simulated from the agreed output alone. Nothing beyond
        the output leaks, but the output itself is not protected.
  \item[Differential privacy.] An individual's influence on a released quantity is
        bounded~\cite{dwork2014}, first applied to recommendation by McSherry and
        Mironov~\cite{mcsherry2009}. This constrains what the \emph{output} reveals, and says
        nothing about who observes the computation. \wally{} occupies an interesting middle
        position, using differential privacy ``to break the linear computation barrier of
        fully-oblivious schemes''.
  \item[Anonymity.] Identity rather than content is hidden, typically by mixing or relaying.
        Weaker against traffic analysis, but cheap and deployed.
\end{description}

The critical point, and the one Section~\ref{sec:open:functionality} returns to, is that these
compose but do not substitute. Cryptographic privacy bounds \emph{who sees the computation}, while
differential privacy bounds \emph{what the result implies}. A system can be cryptographically
perfect and still leak, if its intended output is itself revealing.

\subsection{Axis 3: which pipeline stage is protected}
\label{sec:taxonomy:stage}

Mapping onto Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}: collection, training, serving, delivery. This is the axis
the literature is least explicit about, and the one that most often makes two systems
incomparable. It is also where Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy} is most informative, because the columns
are conspicuously uneven.

\subsection{Axis 4: the adversary}
\label{sec:taxonomy:adversary}

Semi-honest adversaries follow the protocol and try to learn from what they see, while malicious
adversaries may deviate arbitrarily. Orthogonally, a scheme specifies how many parties may be
corrupted, and whether corruption is fixed in advance. Almost every system evaluated at realistic
scale in this survey is semi-honest: \duoram{} tolerates ``a single passive corruption'',
\prac{} assumes ``none of the three parties \dots\ collude'', and \nudge{} protects against ``an
adversary that compromises the entire secret state of one server''. That is a substantive
limitation rather than a technicality, discussed in Section~\ref{sec:open:malicious}.

\subsection{Reading the table}
\label{sec:taxonomy:reading}

Three observations follow from Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}, and each is developed later.

\textbf{The stage column is uneven.} Training and serving are well populated, while \emph{delivery}
is addressed almost exclusively by the PIR line. Only one recommendation system in the table,
\pirsonaplain{}~\cite{pirsona2021}, spans collection, training and delivery together. The
oblivious-memory row group has no stage at all, because those works are primitives that a system
must be built \emph{from}. Section~\ref{sec:both} takes up what this unevenness means.

\textbf{Cost is bought with assumptions, consistently.} The cheapest protocols in each row group
are those assuming non-collusion. This is most visible in PIR, where the authors of
SimplePIR~\cite{simplepir2023} position their single-server throughput as approaching ``the
performance of the fastest two-server private-information-retrieval schemes (which require
non-colluding servers)''. That comparison is worth making precisely because removing the
assumption normally costs something.

\textbf{Semi-honest is the norm at scale.} Every recommendation system in the table is
semi-honest. The exceptions elsewhere are narrow: Hafiz and Henry's PIR is computationally
$1$-private against a malicious server~\cite{hafiz2019}, \compass{} states its guarantee holds
``even if the server is compromised'', and Pika~\cite{pika2022} extends FSS-based function
evaluation to malicious security in the honest-majority setting. Section~\ref{sec:open:malicious}
returns to why this is difficult.
